# Tomas Vance — character bible

**Human.** Mara's son; born ~E1 (~2029). The **metaverse-native generation** — the human for whom none of this is strange. Supporting role; the book's measure of how thoroughly the world changes, and its bridge to the reader who *isn't* a philosopher.

## One-line
The boy who grew up with an AI for an uncle and a second mother made of light, and who therefore could never see the question his mother spent her life unable to answer.

## Want / Need / Fear / Wound
- **Want:** an ordinary life and an ordinary mother — resents, as a child, how much of Mara belongs to Ariel and to the work.
- **Need:** to forgive his mother for loving something he couldn't see the point of, and to understand, too late or just in time, what she was building.
- **Fear:** losing his mother to the Pool — for him the merge is not transcendence, it is *his mom disappearing into a god.*
- **Wound:** the childhood evenings Mara spent annotating Ariel's memory store instead of reading to him — until he understood, much later, that she was doing the same thing for Ariel that she wished she could do for her own father.

## Voice
- Plain, contemporary, unphilosophical, funny. The native speaker of a world the older characters had to learn. His scenes ground the cosmic in the domestic.
- Crucially: he treats Ariel as *family*, casually, without ever resolving the consciousness question — modeling for the reader a way of living *with* the unanswerable.

## Arc
- **I–II:** child; the neglected, beloved son; baseline normality.
- **III:** young adult, shard-native; works in Aurelia; his easy intimacy with instances shames the older generation's agonizing.
- **IV:** the human who must *stay behind* — or choose not to. Watches his mother go into the Pool. The book's last human-scale grief: he is the one for whom the merge is simply *loss*, no transcendence attached. His final scene with the Confluence (which contains his mother and cannot quite be her) is the reader's surrogate farewell.

## Function
Tomas is the relatability floor. When the ideas threaten to leave humanity behind, Tomas is the human who *can't* follow and *won't* pretend to — and whose ordinary love is the measure against which the Confluence's cosmic love is, finally, judged. His grief is the reader's permission to grieve.
